New-store readiness

Cutting new-store training from 14 days to 5

Redesigning how a fast-scaling VR company gets new locations open, ready, and consistent.

RoleProgram strategy, operating-model design, stakeholder alignment, and rollout
Challenge14-day on-site training was the bottleneck on every new-store opening
What I didRebuilt the model from all-live to blended, with async pre-work and local training capacity
Result64% reduction in training time, 39 openings supported, and an estimated $56K in trainer lodging avoided

The challenge

Every new Sandbox VR location opened on the back of a 14-day, fully on-site training program. It worked, but it did not scale.

As the company accelerated, the model became the constraint. Openings jumped from 3 in 2023 to 19 in 2025, with 10 more in the first quarter of 2026 alone. Each opening pulled trainers into two straight weeks of travel and delivery, delayed the date a store could start generating revenue, and left quality dependent on which trainer happened to be in the room.

The math was simple: the old model could not deliver that volume of openings without either burning out the training team or letting consistency slip. Something had to change structurally, not just get faster.

The redesign: protect on-site time for hands-on practice, move everything else earlier or local

My role

I led the redesign from operating problem through field adoption, growing from Field Trainer into the Senior Global L&D Program Manager role during the work. I set the learning strategy, aligned the new model with operations and field leadership, built the supporting content and standards, and created local capability so the system would not depend on one central trainer.

The leadership challenge was as important as the instructional one: decide what could move out of the classroom, establish a consistent readiness bar, earn confidence in a shorter model, and distribute delivery without losing quality.

The approach

Rather than trim the existing 14 days, I rebuilt the model around a simple principle: on-site time is expensive, so protect it for the things that can only happen in person. Everything else moved earlier or moved local.

Shifted foundational knowledge to async pre-work

I built a 54-module eLearning catalog from the ground up, owning instructional design, scripting, filming, voiceover, editing, and LMS deployment with no outside production vendors. Concepts and procedures that used to eat classroom hours became structured pre-work, so trainees arrived on day one already grounded. The on-site days could then focus on hands-on practice and real floor readiness instead of lecture.

Built local training capacity

I established eight certified training stores and developed 12 regional trainers and training leaders, distributing delivery across the network. Certification standards, coaching, and clear ownership let local leaders carry the model while I governed quality across markets. Openings stopped being gated by a small central team's travel calendar.

Standardized the on-site portion

With the foundation handled up front and consistent delivery in place, the in-person program compressed to five focused days that were the same in every market, not dependent on the individual trainer.

The outcome

  • Training time dropped 64%, from 14 days to 5.
  • The model supported 39 location openings between 2023 and Q1 2026 (3 in 2023, 7 in 2024, 19 in 2025, and 10 in Q1 2026), a volume the original approach could not have absorbed.
  • Local capability replaced central dependency. Eight certified training stores and 12 regional trainers and training leaders created capacity closer to each market.
  • An estimated $56,000 in trainer lodging avoided, before counting airfare, per diem, ground travel, or trainer time. This is a directional estimate, not a booked budget reduction.
  • Openings were no longer bottlenecked by trainer travel, freeing the team to support more launches in parallel.
  • Delivery became more consistent across markets, because the foundation was standardized and local trainers were certified to the same bar.

Estimate basis: roughly nine fewer hotel nights per opening across 39 openings, at approximately the 2025 U.S. average nightly rate of $160.
